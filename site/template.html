What counts as low-stress. Streets are rated with the Level of Traffic Stress method from the Mineta Transportation Institute (Mekuria, Furth & Nixon, 2012). "Kids and cautious riders" counts only paths, protected bike lanes, and quiet streets posted 25 mph or less with no more than 2 lanes; crossing a street posted 30 mph or more, or with 3 or more lanes, is stressful unless there is a signal or an all-way stop, and crossing a street posted 35 mph or more or with 4 or more lanes (such as El Camino Real) is stressful even at a signal. "Most adults" also counts painted bike lanes on streets up to 30 mph, quiet streets up to 30 mph, and busier 2-lane streets up to 25 mph, and treats crossings as stressful only at 35 mph or 4 lanes.

The questions. In the quiz, questions 1 to 3, their 4-point answer scale, and the cyclist types come from Jennifer Dill and Nathan McNeil, "Revisiting the Four Types of Cyclists: Findings from a National Survey," Transportation Research Record 2587 (2016), building on Roger Geller's "Four Types of Cyclists" (Portland, 2006); their lead-in ("For each of the following places you could ride a bike, please indicate whether you would be comfortable or uncomfortable biking there") is shortened here, and numbers are written as digits ("20–25 mph" for "twenty to twenty-five miles per hour," "4 lanes" for "four lanes"). Their full method also asks about interest in riding and physical ability, which this quiz leaves out, so it can't place anyone in their fourth type, "no way, no how." Questions 4 to 7 are written for this map, and haven't been tested in a survey. The map's standard is "most adults" only if you'd be comfortable (very or somewhat) with questions 1 and 4 to 7.

Connected. A home counts as having a low-stress route to a station when one exists that is no more than 25% longer than the shortest ride on any street, for homes within a 2-mile ride of the station. Homes are 2020 Census housing units by block. Fixes are tested one at a time.

Sources. Streets from OpenStreetMap contributors (fetched ); posted speed limits from the City of San Mateo's 2026 speed survey; housing units from the 2020 Census; a few speed limits and intersection controls checked in person. Where neither OpenStreetMap nor the survey gives a speed limit, residential streets are assumed to be 25 mph and major streets 30 to 40 mph. Code and data: github.com/mmautner/charts.
