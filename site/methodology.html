Bike islands: San Mateo and Burlingame

How this was made

What counts as low-stress

Streets are rated with the Level of Traffic Stress method from the Mineta Transportation Institute (Mekuria, Furth & Nixon, 2012), simplified to what OpenStreetMap records.

  • Kids and cautious riders counts only paths, protected bike lanes, and quiet streets posted 25 mph or less with no more than 2 lanes. Crossing a street posted 30 mph or more, or with 3 or more lanes, is stressful unless there is a signal or an all-way stop. Crossing a street posted 35 mph or more, or with 4 or more lanes (such as El Camino Real), is stressful even at a signal. That last rule is stricter than Mineta's, which lets any signal make a crossing safe; without it, a few signals on El Camino and Howard Avenue joined neighborhoods across both cities into one island.
  • Most adults also counts painted bike lanes on streets up to 30 mph, quiet streets up to 30 mph, and busier 2-lane streets up to 25 mph, and treats crossings as stressful only at 35 mph or 4 lanes.
  • Flashing beacons for people walking don't count as a control: a person on a bike can't be expected to stop, walk to the button and cross as a pedestrian.

Connected to Caltrain

  • A home counts as having a low-stress route to a station when one exists that is no more than 25% longer than the shortest ride on any street, for homes within a 2-mile ride of the station's platforms.
  • Homes are 2020 Census housing units by block, placed at each block's center and matched to the nearest low-stress street within 150 m.
  • Fixes are tested one at a time: each candidate street segment or crossing is treated as low-stress, and the homes it connects are counted. Combinations of fixes can connect more than the sum.

Riding times

  • After a click, your browser finds the shortest low-stress route from that spot to every street on the island and colors the island by riding time, at an easy 10 mph. Hills, waits at lights and slower riding with children aren't counted. The click and the times stay in your browser; the link you share names only the island.
  • A shared link has no click, so it colors the island by riding time from the Caltrain station it reaches, if any.
  • Street search and "Use my location" also run entirely in your browser: the street list ships with the page, and neither what you type nor your location is sent anywhere.
  • The "What you can do" cards work out which city and San Mateo council district a spot is in from boundaries that ship with the page (City of San Mateo council voting districts; Census 2020 city limits). Council contacts are the same list the crash lookup uses; Burlingame's council is elected citywide, so its email goes to council@burlingame.org.

  • Posted speed limits from the City of San Mateo's 2026 Engineering and Traffic Survey, obtained through a public records request (see San Mateo speeds). Where neither the survey nor OpenStreetMap gives a limit, residential streets are assumed to be 25 mph and major streets 30 to 40 mph.
  • A few speed limits and intersection controls were checked in person or on Google Street View, and two links of the shared-use path to Hillsdale station, which OpenStreetMap draws unconnected, were added from the city's project page. Each is listed with its source in the repository.
  • Housing units from the 2020 Census.

The quiz

The wording of questions 1 to 3, their 4-point answer scale, and the cyclist types come from Jennifer Dill and Nathan McNeil, "Revisiting the Four Types of Cyclists: Findings from a National Survey," Transportation Research Record 2587 (2016), building on Roger Geller's "Four Types of Cyclists" (Portland, 2006). Their lead-in ("For each of the following places you could ride a bike, please indicate whether you would be comfortable or uncomfortable biking there") is shortened here, and numbers are written as digits ("20–25 mph" for "twenty to twenty-five miles per hour," "4 lanes" for "four lanes"). Their full method also asks about interest in riding and physical ability, which this quiz leaves out, so it can't place anyone in their fourth type, "no way, no how." Questions 4 to 7 are written for this map and haven't been tested in a survey. The map's standard is "most adults" only if you'd be comfortable (very or somewhat) with questions 1 and 4 to 7. Photos are from Google Street View.

Limits

  • OpenStreetMap is mapped by volunteers; a missing signal or a wrong lane count changes which streets connect. Golf cart paths, tagged or not, are left out: anything tagged as a cart path, and unnamed paths and service roads inside a golf course.
  • The rating uses speed and lanes, not traffic volume, so a 25 mph street with heavy traffic can still rate low-stress.
  • The map shows where low-stress routes exist, not whether people would ride them.
