Bike islands: San Mateo and Burlingame

How this was made

What counts as low-stress

Streets are rated with the Level of Traffic Stress method from the Mineta Transportation Institute (Mekuria, Furth & Nixon, 2012), simplified to what OpenStreetMap records.

The quiz picks the map that fits you. Mekuria, Furth & Nixon describe the stricter level (LTS 1) as suitable for children, which is why the school figures use it. They describe the other (LTS 2) as suitable for most adults; this map says "experienced riders" because many adults aren't comfortable in a painted bike lane on a 30 mph street.

  • Kids and cautious riders (LTS 1) counts only paths, protected bike lanes, and quiet streets posted 25 mph or less with no more than 2 lanes. Crossing a street posted 30 mph or more, or with 3 or more lanes, is stressful unless there is a signal or an all-way stop. Crossing a street posted 35 mph or more, or with 4 or more lanes (such as El Camino Real), is stressful even at a signal. That last rule is stricter than Mineta's, which lets any signal make a crossing safe; without it, a few signals on El Camino and Howard Avenue joined neighborhoods across both cities into one island.
  • Experienced riders (LTS 1 and 2) also counts painted bike lanes on streets up to 30 mph, quiet streets up to 30 mph, and busier 2-lane streets up to 25 mph, and treats crossings as stressful only at 35 mph or 4 lanes.
  • Where the city's speed survey counted daily traffic (San Mateo's surveyed streets), busy streets rate as more stressful to ride on, following the Greater Madison MPO's 2023 table, which added traffic volume to the Mineta method in line with NACTO guidance: on a street with one lane each way posted 25 to 30 mph, more than 3,000 vehicles a day is stressful even for experienced riders. Volume can only raise a street's stress, and it doesn't change how crossings are rated. Burlingame's streets aren't adjusted yet: its survey has been requested.
  • A signal or all-way stop counts at the intersection it controls. OpenStreetMap often draws a signal or stop sign on each approach, a few metres before the intersection; the map moves it to the first intersection within 30 m (25 m for stop signs) in the direction it faces. Before November 2026 the map missed these, and all-way stops entirely, so it treated signalized crossings such as El Camino Real at 20th Avenue as uncontrolled.
  • On a divided road, drawn as a separate one-way roadway for each direction, the street's lanes are both roadways' together: two lanes each way is a 4-lane street, to ride on and to cross. A roundabout and the split legs that meet it don't count as a divided road.
  • Flashing beacons for people walking don't count as a control: a person on a bike can't be expected to stop, walk to the button and cross as a pedestrian.

Connected to an elementary school

  • Schools are the California Department of Education's open public elementary schools. A school is reached where a low-stress street comes within 40 m of its grounds as OpenStreetMap draws them, or, for the 14 schools with no grounds mapped, within 150 m of the directory's location.
  • A home counts as having a low-stress route to a school when one reaches any public elementary school and is no more than 25% longer than the shortest ride to the nearest one, for homes within a 2-mile ride of a school.
  • Kids are 2020 Census residents under 18 (total population minus adults, by block), placed like homes. The Census doesn't count ages 5 to 10 by block, so teenagers are included.
  • Attendance areas aren't used: the nearest school may not be the assigned one, and choice and immersion schools count like any other.
  • Fixes are still ranked by the homes they connect to Caltrain, not to schools.

Connected to Caltrain

  • A home counts as having a low-stress route to a station when one exists that is no more than 25% longer than the shortest ride on any street, for homes within a 2-mile ride of the station's platforms.
  • Routes follow one-way streets, and a home counts only if it has a low-stress route there and a low-stress route back: the longer of the two rides is compared with the longer of the two shortest legal rides on any street. Alleys and parking aisles count as two-way, as does a one-way street with a contra-flow bike lane. An island is where you can ride from any point to any other and back. The same rule applies to schools and everyday places.
  • Homes are 2020 Census housing units by block, placed at each block's center and matched to the nearest low-stress street within 150 m.
  • Fixes are tested one at a time: each candidate street segment or crossing is treated as low-stress, and the homes it connects are counted. Combinations of fixes can connect more than the sum.
  • Each street fix is trimmed to the stretch the connected homes' routes actually use, and named by its cross streets; the trimmed stretch connects the same homes as the whole street. A fix whose routes only cross the street is shown as that crossing.
  • For each fix and each piece in the way, the map works out what would make it count under its own rules: it changes one thing at a time (speed limit, lanes, a painted or protected bike lane, whether it's a local street, daily traffic) and re-rates the street or crossing, keeping the smallest changes that pass. Where a speed or lane count is assumed rather than posted or mapped, or there's no daily traffic count, it says so. This is what the map's rating would need, not an engineering recommendation; the city chooses the tool.

The city's own projects

  • San Mateo has already adopted many fixes: the 70 projects of its 2020 Bicycle Master Plan, and a few bike-related items in its 2012 Pedestrian Master Plan and its 2022 plan for walking to Caltrain. A plan doesn't mean a project is funded or scheduled, but an adopted project is what the city can seek grants for, so it's the easiest thing to ask for.
  • Each project is tested like a fix: its streets get the treatment the plan names (a bike lane, a protected lane or path, a bike boulevard taken as a traffic-calmed 20 mph street, or fewer traffic lanes), and the homes it connects are counted. A piece that still wouldn't count for the rider you've chosen (a painted lane on a street too busy for kids, say) is left out.
  • Crossings count only where the project makes them safe. A bike lane along a busy street doesn't make crossing that street any easier, so where a project would help only if crossing its own street were made safe too, the panel says so.
  • When a city project would connect at least three-quarters of what this map's best single fix would, the panel leads with the city's project; otherwise it leads with the fix, and says whether that fix is in any of the city's plans. Costs are the plan's 2020 estimates.

Riding times

  • After a click, your browser finds the shortest low-stress route from that spot to every street on the island and colors the island by riding time, at an easy 10 mph. Hills, waits at lights and slower riding with children aren't counted, and these times don't yet follow one-way streets. The click and the times stay in your browser; the link you share names only the island.
  • A shared link has no click, so it colors the island by riding time from the Caltrain station it reaches, if any.
  • Street search and "Use my location" also run entirely in your browser: the street list ships with the page, and neither what you type nor your location is sent anywhere.
  • The "What you can do" cards work out which city a spot is in from Census 2020 city limits that ship with the page, to link to that city's service requests.
  • Islands and fixes have their own share pages, so a link previews with that island's or fix's map and numbers. They contain no location beyond the island or fix itself.

The quiz

The wording of questions 1 to 3, their 4-point answer scale, and the cyclist types come from Jennifer Dill and Nathan McNeil, "Revisiting the Four Types of Cyclists: Findings from a National Survey," Transportation Research Record 2587 (2016), building on Roger Geller's "Four Types of Cyclists" (Portland, 2006). Their lead-in ("For each of the following places you could ride a bike, please indicate whether you would be comfortable or uncomfortable biking there") is shortened here, and numbers are written as digits ("20–25 mph" for "twenty to twenty-five miles per hour," "4 lanes" for "four lanes"). Their full method also asks about interest in riding and physical ability, which this quiz leaves out, so it can't place anyone in their fourth type, "no way, no how." Questions 4 to 7 are written for this map and haven't been tested in a survey. The map is "experienced riders" only if you'd be comfortable (very or somewhat) with questions 1 and 4 to 7. Photos are from Google Street View.

Limits

  • OpenStreetMap is mapped by volunteers; a missing signal or a wrong lane count changes which streets connect. Golf cart paths, tagged or not, are left out: anything tagged as a cart path, and unnamed paths and service roads inside a golf course.
  • Traffic volume is only known for streets in San Mateo's speed survey; elsewhere, a 25 mph street with heavy traffic can still rate low-stress.
  • The map shows where low-stress routes exist, not whether people would ride them.
